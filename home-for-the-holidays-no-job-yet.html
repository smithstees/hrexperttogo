For College Students and New Grads

Home for the Holidays With No Job Yet: How to Handle the Questions

By Ty Smith, SHRM-CP | HR Expert to go | Job Search

You know the question is coming. Somewhere between the turkey and the pie, an aunt, a grandparent, or a family friend will lean in and ask, "So, any luck with the job search?" If the honest answer is "not yet," that question can make you want to skip the whole gathering.

Please don't. These conversations are uncomfortable, but they are also one of the few times of year when a room full of people who want good things for you is asking how they can help. You can use that. The key is walking in with a plan for what you will say instead of making it up on the spot.

Decide Your Answer Before You Walk In

Most of the discomfort comes from being caught off guard. When you have nothing ready, you either over-explain or shut the conversation down, and neither one feels good afterward.

Prepare one or two sentences that cover what you are looking for and one thing you are doing about it. Keep it short, true, and specific.

  • "I'm looking for entry-level marketing roles in Raleigh or Charlotte. I've had two first-round interviews this month."
  • "I'm focused on accounting jobs at mid-size firms, and I'm working on getting my resume in better shape before the January hiring push."
  • "I'm still figuring out which direction makes the most sense for my degree, so I'm talking to people who work in a few different fields."

None of those say "I have a job." All of them show you are moving. That is usually enough to change the tone of the conversation.

Turn the Question Into a Networking Ask

When a relative asks about your search, they have just opened a door. Instead of defending yourself, ask them a question back. People generally like talking about their own work, and your relatives know more people than you think: coworkers, neighbors, people from church, former classmates, clients, and friends of friends.

Try something like this:

  • "I'm still looking. You've been at your company a long time. What do you think they look for when they hire someone new?"
  • "I'm trying to get into healthcare administration. Do you know anyone who works in a hospital or a clinic office?"
  • "Would you be comfortable introducing me to them? Even a 15-minute call after the holidays would help me a lot."

Be specific about what you want. "Let me know if you hear of anything" rarely leads anywhere. "Could you introduce me to your neighbor who works in HR at the hospital?" gives them something they can actually do.

If someone says yes, follow up within a week while it is still fresh. Send a short text or email thanking them and reminding them of the introduction they offered. Do not make them chase you to help you.

Ask for Help With the Cost of Coaching

Around the holidays, relatives also ask a second question: "What do you want this year?" If you are serious about your search, you can answer that honestly too.

Many families would rather spend money on something useful than on another sweater. If career coaching would help you, it is reasonable to say so. Be clear about what it is and why you want it.

  • "Honestly, the most helpful thing would be help with my job search. I'd like to work with a career coach on my resume and interviews."
  • "If anyone wants to go in together on a gift, I'd love a coaching session more than anything else this year."

Name the specific thing you want and what it costs so nobody has to guess. If several relatives want to contribute, one person can buy it and the others can chip in with them. Asking for this is not begging. You are telling the people who care about you what would actually help, which is exactly what they asked.

Steer the Conversation Toward What Is Going Well

You are more than your job search, and you do not owe anyone a progress report over dinner. Once you have given your short answer, it is fine to move the conversation somewhere else. The easiest way is to share something that is going well.

  • A class, project, or presentation you are proud of
  • A skill you picked up or a certification you are working toward
  • Feedback you got in an interview, even if you did not get the job
  • Your part-time job, volunteer work, or something you are learning on your own

You can make the turn in one sentence: "Still working on it. The best part of this fall was actually the capstone project I finished. We built a marketing plan for a real local business." Then keep talking about that. Most people will follow your lead.

If someone keeps pushing, you are allowed to set a boundary kindly: "I appreciate you asking. I'm giving myself today off from the search, but I'll let you know when I have news." Then ask about them, or go help in the kitchen.

For Parents: How to Help at the Table

If your graduate is coming home without a job, you can make the gathering easier. Before everyone arrives, ask what they are comfortable with you sharing. Some graduates want the family to know they are looking so people can help. Others would rather not discuss it at all.

Do not announce their situation for them or tell the table how worried you are. If a relative starts pressing, help change the subject. If you know someone in their field, offer the introduction privately rather than in front of everyone.

And if relatives ask you what your graduate needs this year, you can point them toward something useful.

The Bottom Line

Going home without a job is hard, but you do not have to spend the holidays dodging questions. Walk in with a short, honest answer. Turn curious relatives into introductions. Tell the people who ask what would actually help. Then talk about what is going well and enjoy being home.
