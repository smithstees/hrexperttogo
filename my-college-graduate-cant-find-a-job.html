For Parents of New Graduates

My college graduate can't find a job. What to do next.

By Ty Smith, SHRM-CP|HR Expert to Go|Job Search Guidance

Watching a capable young adult send applications into the void is rough. If your college graduate can't find a job, it is tempting to jump straight to panic, pressure, or a giant spreadsheet. Take a breath. A slow start is common, but a stalled search deserves a closer look.

The useful question is not whether your child has tried hard enough. It is whether their search has a clear target, a credible story, and enough real human contact to create momentum.

Is this normal or a warning sign?

A job search can take longer than anyone wants, particularly when a graduate is still deciding what kind of role they want or is applying in a competitive location. No offer after a few weeks is not proof that something is wrong. No interviews after a long run of applications, however, is a signal to review the approach.

Look for patterns rather than a single calendar date. Are they applying only to a narrow set of jobs? Are they hearing back but struggling in interviews? Are they applying broadly because they do not know what fits? Those are solvable problems, and they call for different fixes.

The three things stalled searches almost always share

First, the target is too vague. “Anything in business” is understandable, but it does not help a hiring manager see fit. A search works better when the graduate can name a few role families, industries, and locations worth pursuing.

Second, the materials tell a student story instead of a work story. Coursework, internships, projects, campus leadership, and part-time work can all demonstrate useful skills, but the resume has to translate them. A focused resume coaching session can help turn experience into evidence rather than a list of duties.

Third, the search lives entirely online. Job boards matter, but applications are only one channel. Alumni, former supervisors, faculty, family friends, and professional groups can provide context, introductions, and information about roles that are not obvious from a posting.

What actually moves a stuck search forward

Start by narrowing the target. Pick a manageable group of roles and build a simple weekly rhythm: tailored applications, outreach conversations, follow-up, and interview preparation. The job search strategy service is built around making that kind of plan practical.

Then practice the story. A graduate should be able to explain what they want to do, what they have already done that is relevant, and why a particular role makes sense. This is not about inventing polish. It is about making the connection clear.

Parents can be most helpful by asking specific questions: What role are you targeting this week? Which conversation was most useful? What part of the process feels unclear? That keeps support focused without turning the living room into a second interview panel.

If you are searching “help my child find a job after college” or “college graduate cannot find a job,” you are not alone. Those phrases often reflect a real concern, but they do not identify the obstacle by themselves. That is why a short review of the search is more useful than pushing for another batch of applications.

When to bring in a career coach

A coach can be useful when effort is high but direction is missing, when the graduate keeps getting interviews without offers, or when the parent-child dynamic has made the topic hard to discuss. A neutral outside perspective is often easier to hear.

Career coaching for college students can cover direction, job-search materials, interviews, and offer decisions. It is not job placement, and it does not replace mental health support if discouragement has become more serious. It is practical help with the parts of the search a new graduate may not have learned in school.

The next 30 days

For the next month, aim for a clearer search rather than more frantic activity. Choose target roles, revise the resume around those roles, schedule a few conversations, and prepare for the questions that come up most often. Review what is working each week and adjust.

“My son graduated college and can't get a job” is a hard sentence to say out loud. Your child being stuck at the starting line does not mean their degree was a mistake. It means the search needs a better system. A little structure can make the next steps feel much less mysterious.
