For Parents of New Graduates

My College Graduate Still Doesn't Have a Job. Should I Be Worried?

By Ty Smith, SHRM-CP | HR Expert to Go | Career Coaching

Graduation was months ago. The cap and gown are packed away, the group chats from senior year have gone quiet, and your student is still refreshing job boards at the kitchen table. You are proud of them. You are also, if you are honest, starting to worry.

You are not overreacting, and you are not the only parent lying awake wondering if something is wrong. But before the worry turns into panic, or into a conversation your grad will resent, it helps to know what is actually normal, what is not, and what to do about it either way.

What "Normal" Actually Looks Like Right Now

Here is something most families do not realize: the job search timeline you remember is not the one your graduate is living through.

Entry-level hiring has slowed and gotten more competitive in recent years. Postings that once drew dozens of applicants now draw hundreds, and many companies have added extra interview rounds and longer decision cycles along the way. A search that takes three to six months after graduation is common. Five to eight is not unusual in a tighter market or a competitive field. That is not a reflection of your grad's talent or work ethic. It is the market they are stepping into.

So if it has been eight or ten weeks, take a breath. If it has been four or five months and your grad still has a clear strategy, is applying with intention, and is getting some interviews, that is a normal search taking its normal course, not a crisis.

The Signs That Actually Warrant Concern

Time alone is not the red flag. These are.

Watch for a search that has become pure volume with no strategy, dozens of applications going out to jobs that do not really fit, with no clear thread connecting them. Watch for silence after silence, where your grad is applying but almost never hearing back at all, which usually points to a resume or application problem rather than bad luck. Watch for interviews that go nowhere, where they are getting first-round conversations but never advancing, which usually means the interview itself needs work. And watch for withdrawal, when the search stops looking like effort and starts looking like avoidance, with fewer applications, more time in bed, and a shorter fuse when you ask how it is going.

That last one is worth sitting with. A stalled job search does not just cost time. It chips away at confidence, and a grad who feels like a failure will often search less, not more, which only extends the very timeline that is discouraging them. If you are seeing withdrawal alongside the search, the conversation you need to have is less about job strategy and more about how they are doing.

Why "Just Keep Applying" Does Not Work

It is the advice every parent reaches for, and it is usually the wrong one.

Applying to more jobs does not fix a resume that is not getting past applicant tracking systems. It does not fix interview answers that ramble or undersell what your grad actually brings. It does not fix a search with no clear target, where they are applying to marketing roles on Monday and operations roles on Wednesday with no strategy behind either. More volume on a broken process just produces more rejection, faster.

What actually moves a stalled search is diagnosis, figuring out specifically where it is breaking down, and then fixing that one thing.

What You Can Do (and What You Probably Cannot)

You can absolutely help. You can be a sounding board, cover the wifi bill a little longer, and remind your grad that this is a season, not a life sentence. What is much harder for a parent to do is sit down and tell your child, with useful specificity, that their resume is not landing, that their interview answers are missing the mark, or that their search strategy needs to change.

Even if you could see the problem clearly, they are far more likely to hear it, and act on it, from someone who is not you. That is not a knock on your relationship. It is just how feedback works between a parent and an adult child who is already feeling fragile about this exact topic.

The Bottom Line

A slow job search is not proof that something is wrong with your graduate. But a stalled one, left alone too long, can turn into something harder to recover from: lost confidence, mounting frustration, and a search that keeps repeating the same mistakes.
